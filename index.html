THE PRACTITIONER PROBLEM

Claims, clinical records, ADT events, and external sources did not inherently share a single patient identity or consistent meaning. The platform needed to resolve records across sources into a coherent, patient-centered model before downstream reporting could be trusted.

WHAT I BUILT

I built the patient-centered ontology and identity-resolution relationships across sources in Palantir Foundry, then used Python, SQL, and its low-code development environment to create an enterprise reporting suite. The work combined data semantics, transformation logic and usable analytics experiences.

PRODUCT + TECHNICAL JUDGMENT

The foundational choice was to reconcile patient identity and establish common entity relationships upstream, rather than reproduce source-specific matching and definitions in isolated dashboards. This made the patient, rather than any single source system, the organizing concept.

OUTCOME

The resulting reporting suite supported enterprise quality and performance reporting. Related work included a HEDIS-based quality engine and risk-adjustment pipeline. The repo includes diagnostic quality and coverage analyses, but no verified before-and-after improvement directly attributable to this reporting suite.

THE DEVELOPER PROBLEM

Earlier representations mixed document-level artifacts with clinical events, packed multiple coding systems into concatenated fields, and left customers to perform parsing, mapping and deduplication. That shifted platform complexity downstream.

THE PRODUCT PROPOSAL

A draft FLAT 2.0 model reframed encounters as discrete visits, retained original source codes while introducing explicit enriched terminology fields, standardized missing values, and preserved provenance through source-reference relationships.

VALIDATING THE TRADEOFF

I compared customer ETL steps against the old and proposed delivery models. The analysis identified tasks the platform could eliminate or simplify, including some normalization lookups, while being explicit about the transformation and business logic customers would still need.

STATUS & IMPACT

These are documented design proposals and investigations, not claims that every proposed capability was launched. Separately, I led the Signal product to approximately $2.4M ARR; that revenue is not attributed to the FLAT 2.0 design.

Evidence: semantic-model draft, FLAT migration ETL-impact review, and data quality investigation in jordan-sandbox. Internal findings have been generalized; draft work is explicitly labeled.

THE QUALITY CHALLENGE

A plausible summary can still omit important findings or misrepresent its source. The product approach emphasized traceable citations, explicit evaluation dimensions, and clinical reviewers who could inspect underlying records.

THE INTERNAL DEVELOPER EXPERIENCE

The internal platform brought together production and experimental summary reviews. It offered a flat-data viewer, shared human/LLM evaluation records, and experiment workflows with configurable prompts and criteria. The documented runner used Prefect and stored reproducible experiment outputs in GCS.

THE EVALUATION METHOD

The documented methodology starts with expert review of real failures, groups recurring failure modes, and builds focused code checks or LLM judges. Judges must be evaluated against human labels, including disagreements and false passes; automation does not replace clinical judgment.

OWNERSHIP & MATURITY

I contributed early data-viewer code and set product direction. The evaluation platform was predominantly engineered by the data science team. An interactive patient assistant and customer-initiated summary refresh were separately documented as proposals, not represented here as shipped products. This case study does not claim a quantified reduction in review time because a verified baseline and post-launch measurement were not located.

003 / HOW I WORK

Technical depth matters most when it changes the product decisions you make.

Move between the system and the user.

01

Start with the practitioner.

Understand the workflows, friction, and workarounds of the people using the platform. Define the problem before selecting the solution.

02

Go deep enough to decide.

Work with engineering on architecture, schemas, API contracts, data quality, and system tradeoffs. Build directly when that creates clarity.

03

Design for trust and adoption.

Ship reusable foundations, articulate why they matter, and evaluate success through user outcomes rather than a list of delivered features.
